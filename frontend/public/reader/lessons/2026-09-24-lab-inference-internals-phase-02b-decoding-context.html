Lab card: stream freeze, schema tax, and the 400

One Saturday page: run the three Part B simulators and the two live schema runs, record the numbers, break each on purpose.

Three client-side failures hide in a working agent loop until you build the tests that find them.

Last weekend you built the scheduler that shares one GPU. The agents that call it look fine: they stream, they emit structured output, they remember the conversation. Your evals pass. But the failures you proved in Part B do not show up in evals that pass. A stream that freezes mid-answer while HTTP stays connected. A strict JSON schema that still breaks the parser on ticket number nine. A retrieval loop that dies with a 400 at turn 42 because the context window filled in silence.

This lab makes all three fail on your machine, on purpose. Three runs are offline, using a mock SSE feed and recorded model output, so no key is needed. The two live runs cost about $0.02 in total. You will fill one table: worst stream gap and time to first token at two token budgets, the constraint tax on 12 tickets, the turn where compaction must trigger, and one forced backstop with its log line. The question the lab leaves open: which of these numbers survives when your loop runs against a real provider's production limits, and which only exists on your laptop.

Averages hide all three failures; only the worst gap, the truncated column, and the token count show them.

The build

Five runs produce the numbers this lab card asks you to keep.

Each step names the concept it exercises, the command, and what you should see. Run them in order. The live steps are marked UNVERIFIED against a live provider: record what you actually see, with the date.


  • A small budget buys smooth streams and costs TTFT. A large budget does the reverse. Run both and compare the chunked row. 
    python phase02_internals/batching_sim.py --token-budget 512
python phase02_internals/batching_sim.py --token-budget 8192
    Look for mean TTFT 1,094ms and worst gap 35ms at 512. Look for 908ms and 265ms at 8,192. The two numbers move in opposite directions. That opposition is the trade itself.

  • You ran this in "Before you start". If the last line was "all self-tests passed", move on. If not, stop here. A broken parser would fake the constraint tax in the next two steps.

  • The same 12 tickets go through three modes: prompt, json, strict. The run costs at most about $0.02. 
    python phase02_internals/structured_lab.py --model gpt-4.1-mini --role cheap
    Expect at least one parse failure in prompt mode and zero in json and strict. The accuracy difference between prompt and strict is your constraint tax on this task. It can be negative. A negative value means the schema helped.

  • The fix for the tax is one free-text field placed first in the schema, so the model reasons before the constrained fields are chosen. 
    python phase02_internals/structured_lab.py --model gpt-4.1-mini --role cheap --reasoning-field
    Watch output tokens, cost, and latency rise. Accuracy may or may not rise. Compare with step 3 and decide whether the trade is worth it on this task.

  • The trigger is a number you choose. Three runs show what the choice does. 
    python phase02_internals/compactor.py --simulate --turns 40
python phase02_internals/compactor.py --simulate --turns 40 --trigger-ratio 0.3
python phase02_internals/compactor.py --simulate --turns 40 --keep-last 10
    First run: the trigger fires at turn 25, the prompt drops from 19,409 to 2,270 tokens, with 45 turns summarised and 4 kept verbatim. Second run: the trigger moves to 9,600 tokens and fires three times, at turns 13, 23, and 33. Third run: one compaction at turn 25, down to 4,604 tokens, with 10 kept verbatim.
E8, E9, and E10 were not run against a live provider when this material was written. The flags were checked against the shipped scripts. What you see is the data; the numbers above are the expectation.

Break it

Breaking it on purpose proves the failure is real and the catch works.

The build steps kept everything green. These two steps make each failure happen on purpose, so you see the exact shape your guardrails have to catch. Each break teaches one concept from the sections above.

  • Truncate an answer that follows the schema, from E10 in the lab. The concept: a strict schema guarantees legal tokens, never a complete answer.

    python phase02_internals/structured_lab.py --model gpt-4.1-mini --role cheap --modes strict --reasoning-field --max-tokens 15

    Expected: the truncated column fills up. Parse failures appear, even though every answer followed the schema until it stopped. Strict mode does not protect you here. Only finish_reason sees what happened. This run is unverified against a live provider, so record what you actually see, with the date.

  • Reproduce Phase 1 E7, then watch the backstop catch it, from E12 in the lab. The concept: the trigger is the fix, the backstop is the second layer.

    python phase02_internals/compactor.py --simulate --turns 60 --no-compaction
    python phase02_internals/compactor.py --simulate --turns 12 --inject-overflow 5

    Expected: the first run stops at turn 42 with 32,635 tokens and the line "STOP: prompt exceeds the window. This is the E7 failure." The second run fails on call 5. The backstop compacts from 3,849 to 2,270 tokens, one retry succeeds, and the summary reports backstops: 1. That one line is a bug report on your trigger. It tells you to lower --trigger-ratio.

Done when

Six measured numbers and three checks decide whether the lab worked, not the logs.

You are done when your table holds all six numbers, one per row, each with its source command. Then run the three checks below against what you recorded. Each one catches a specific way the run can go wrong while still looking finished.

Check 1. Does your budget pair show the trade? At budget 512 the chunked row must show TTFT worse and worst gap better than at 8,192.
Answer sketch

Expected: 1,094ms TTFT and 35ms worst gap at 512; 908ms and 265ms at 8,192. TTFT and gap must move in opposite directions. If both moved the same way, something in the run changed besides the budget. Rerun both commands before you trust either number.

Check 2. In the --max-tokens 15 run, did strict mode produce parse failures with zero schema violations?
Answer sketch

Yes, that is the expected shape. Every truncated answer followed the schema until it stopped, so the mask did its job. The failures come from length, not legality. This is the strict-mode limit from section 2.5: the schema controls which tokens are legal, never when generation stops. If your run shows zero parse failures here, the truncation did not happen, so check that --max-tokens 15 was actually applied.

Check 3. Did the --inject-overflow 5 run end with backstops: 1 and a successful retry?
Answer sketch

The run must survive call 5: the backstop compacts 3,849 to 2,270 tokens, one retry succeeds, the summary reports backstops: 1. If the run died at the overflow, the backstop never fired, and your output counts as a reproduction of E7, not as a pass on E12.

One live question stays open after the table is filled. Compare the first-call column with the mean latency column in your strict-mode run.

Reading the first-call gap. A first call far above the mean means your provider compiles each new schema before it can answer. A gap near zero matches OpenAI's current documentation for models that are not fine-tuned.
Answer sketch

There is no correct value to match here. The course marks E8, E9 and E10 as unverified against a live provider, so your number is the measurement. Write down which of the two shapes you saw, and the date you saw it. That dated record is what protects you when the provider's behaviour changes later.

If you have more time

Silence proves nothing: count the dangerous condition, not only the damage.

Your backstop never fired in the runs above. That tells you nothing about whether it works. This week's article, "Did the fix work, or did the bug go quiet?", names the trap: a rare failure and a fixed failure look identical in a quiet month. Tailscale watched six silent weeks pass with no fix at all, so when their fix shipped, they added a counter for the dangerous condition itself. It fired once, with no damage. That firing was the proof.

  • Produce the deceptive calm. Run: python phase02_internals/compactor.py --simulate --turns 60 Compaction is on, so the run finishes with zero errors and zero backstops. That clean output is the deceptive calm: it fits both "the trigger works" and "the backstop is dead code". Note it down as the zero-evidence baseline.
  • Write the one line that is evidence. Go back to your Break 2 output, where the overflow was injected and the run ended with backstops: 1 and a successful retry. Write one line from it: the condition fired once, and no damage followed. Compare that line with the clean X1 run. Only one of them proves the backstop works.
  • Apply it to your own system. Pick one quiet guardrail in your multi-agent system: your overflow backstop or a parser check. Next to the failure counter it already has, add a counter for the condition it guards against. An overflow attempt. A parse retry. When the condition counter rises and the failure counter stays at zero, you have evidence. This is Checkpoint question 4 in miniature.
The pattern. Every guardrail needs two counters: how often its condition fired, and how often damage followed. The ratio is the health of the fix. Silence in both is the shape you cannot read.

A firing condition counter with zero damage proves the fix; silence does not.

A clean run after a fix fits "fixed" and "dormant" equally well, so it verifies nothing.
